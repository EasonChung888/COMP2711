About 20 minutes · Basic proof techniques

Choose a proof opening that gives you useful information

A proof method tells you what to assume first and what endpoint to seek. After that, definitions and algebra do the ordinary work.

Your concrete goal

You will choose among direct proof, contraposition, contradiction, and biconditional proof, then write a complete proof that begins with the correct assumption and ends with the required conclusion.

Plain-English intuition: choose the easier doorway

  • If the hypothesis immediately gives a useful definition, go directly.
  • If the conclusion’s negation has a useful form, prove the contrapositive.
  • If assuming failure creates incompatible facts, use contradiction.
  • If the claim says “if and only if,” prove both directions.

These methods do not compete: a biconditional proof may use a direct argument in one direction and contraposition in the other.

The four proof templates

GoalOpening assumptionRequired ending
Directly prove p → qAssume p.Derive q.
Prove p → q by contrapositionAssume ¬q.Derive ¬p.
Prove statement p by contradictionAssume ¬p.Derive r ∧ ¬r, or another impossibility.
Prove p ↔ qFirst assume p; later assume q.Prove both p → q and q → p.
Definitions are engines. For an integer n: even means n = 2k; odd means n = 2k + 1, for some integer k. A rational number has form a/b with integers a,b and b ≠ 0.

Worked example 1: direct proof

Theorem. If integer n is odd, then n² is odd.

  1. Assume n is odd. This is the hypothesis.
  2. By the definition of odd, n = 2k + 1 for some integer k.
  3. Square and rearrange: n² = (2k+1)² = 4k² + 4k + 1 = 2(2k²+2k)+1.
  4. Because 2k²+2k is an integer, n² has the form 2m+1. Therefore n² is odd.

The last sentence matters: the algebra only becomes a proof when you reconnect the final form to the definition.

Worked example 2: contraposition

Theorem. If 3n+2 is odd, then integer n is odd.

  1. The contrapositive is: if n is even, then 3n+2 is even.
  2. Assume n is even, so n=2k for some integer k.
  3. Then 3n+2 = 3(2k)+2 = 2(3k+1).
  4. Since 3k+1 is an integer, 3n+2 is even. The contrapositive is true, so the original conditional is true.

Do not assume the original hypothesis here. A contrapositive proof starts with the negation of the original conclusion.

Worked example 3: contradiction

Theorem. √2 is irrational.

  1. Assume the opposite: √2 is rational. Then √2=a/b for integers a,b with no common factor and b ≠ 0.
  2. Squaring gives 2b²=a², so a² is even. Hence a is even; write a=2c.
  3. Substitute: 2b²=(2c)²=4c², so b²=2c². Thus b is even.
  4. Both a and b are divisible by 2, contradicting the choice that they have no common factor. Therefore √2 is irrational.
Why does an even square imply an even integer?

Its contrapositive was proved above: an odd integer has an odd square. Therefore, if a² is not odd, a is not odd.

Worked example 4: biconditional proof

Theorem. An integer n is odd if and only if n² is odd.

Forward direction, n odd → n² odd: this is exactly the direct proof in Worked example 1.

Reverse direction, n² odd → n odd: prove the contrapositive. If n is even, then n=2k for an integer k. Hence n²=4k²=2(2k²), which is even. Therefore the reverse conditional holds. Since both directions hold, the biconditional holds.

Common mistakes

  • Proving the converse: for p → q, proving q → p proves a different statement.
  • Wrong contradiction opening: for a conditional, assume p ∧ ¬q, not merely ¬q.
  • One-sided biconditional: proving only p → q earns only half the logical task.
  • Stopping at algebra: explicitly state why the final expression matches “even,” “odd,” “rational,” or the requested property.

Two short checks

Check 1. To prove “if n² is odd, then n is odd,” which opening is most natural?

Hint

The contrapositive is “if n is even, then n² is even.”

Check 2. Complete a direct proof

Prove that the sum of two rational numbers is rational. Begin from the formal definition, track nonzero denominators, and reconnect your result to the definition.

Hint

Write x=a/b and y=c/d, where b,d ≠ 0. Find one fraction for x+y.

Worked answer - open only after trying

Let x=a/b and y=c/d, where a,b,c,d are integers and b,d ≠ 0. Then x+y=(ad+bc)/(bd). The numerator ad+bc and denominator bd are integers, and bd ≠ 0. Hence x+y is rational.

Takeaway

A proof method fixes the opening and target; definitions supply the bridge between them.
