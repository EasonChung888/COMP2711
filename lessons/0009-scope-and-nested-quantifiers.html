About 18 minutes · Scope and dependency

Read quantifiers as choices made in order

Parentheses show where a quantifier controls a variable. With nested quantifiers, the order tells us whether a later choice may depend on an earlier one.

Your concrete goal

You can mark bound and free variables, decide whether an expression is a proposition, and translate or interpret two-variable statements while preserving who may depend on whom.

Plain-English intuition: a choice game

Read quantifiers from left to right as instructions in a game.

∀x∃y R(x,y)

An opponent chooses x; then you may choose a matching y. Your y may change when x changes.

∃y∀x R(x,y)

You must choose one fixed y first; that same value must work for every later x.

Dependency test: ask whether the existential witness is allowed to depend on the universally chosen value.

Formal definitions: binding and scope

  • A quantifier binds its variable inside its scope.
  • A variable occurrence not bound by a quantifier and not assigned a value is free.
  • All variables must be bound or assigned before the expression is a proposition.
  • Quantifiers have higher precedence than logical connectives. Therefore ∀x P(x) ∨ Q(x) is read as (∀x P(x)) ∨ Q(x), leaving the last x free.

Use parentheses to make the intended scope explicit:

∀x(P(x) ∨ Q(x))

Now both occurrences of x are inside the scope of ∀x.

Worked example 1: find every free variable

Consider ∃x(P(x) ∧ Q(x,y)).

  1. The scope of ∃x is the entire parenthesized expression.
  2. Both occurrences of x lie inside that scope, so they are bound.
  3. No quantifier binds y, so y is free.
  4. The expression is not yet a proposition. Prefixing ∀y or ∃y, or assigning a value to y, closes it.

Likely beginner error: seeing a quantifier anywhere and assuming every variable is bound. A quantifier binds only its own variable, only inside its scope.

Worked example 2: order changes the truth value

Domain: all real numbers. Compare two statements about x + y = 0.

  1. ∀x∃y(x + y = 0) is true. After any x is chosen, choose y = −x.
  2. Notice that the witness changes with x: for x = 2, use y = −2; for x = 5, use y = −5.
  3. ∃y∀x(x + y = 0) is false. It asks for one fixed real y that cancels every real x.

This is the central order example on L02, page 37. 2024 Spring Midterm, Problem 3 tests the same dependency idea.

Translation pattern: “there is no largest real number”

  1. Take an arbitrary real x: begin with ∀x.
  2. A larger real may depend on x: continue with ∃y.
  3. State the relationship: y > x.

∀x∃y(y > x)

A concrete witness strategy is y = x + 1. This translation appeared in 2019 Midterm, Problem 2(e), worth 3 of the problem’s 12 points.

Common mistakes

  • Ignoring parentheses: the scope may end before a later connective.
  • Reusing a variable carelessly: inner quantifiers can shadow an outer variable and make a formula hard to read.
  • Swapping mixed quantifiers: ∀x∃y usually does not mean ∃y∀x.
  • Forgetting dependency: in ∀x∃y, one fixed witness is not required; a method for choosing y from x is enough.

Hint

Takeaway

Scope tells you which variable occurrences are controlled; order tells you which choices may depend on earlier choices.
