Reference 02 · L03 Inference and Proofs

Rules that move a proof.

An inference rule turns premises you already have into a justified new line. A proof template tells you what to assume first and what the final line must establish.

1 · Eight propositional inference rules

Modus ponens

p
p → q
∴ q

Trigger a conditional with its hypothesis.

Modus tollens

¬q
p → q
∴ ¬p

Deny the consequence, then deny the hypothesis.

Hypothetical syllogism

p → q
q → r
∴ p → r

Join two compatible implication links.

Disjunctive syllogism

p ∨ q
¬p
∴ q

Remove an impossible branch of an or.

Addition

p
∴ p ∨ q

A true statement makes an inclusive or true.

Simplification

p ∧ q
∴ p

Extract either true component of an and.

Conjunction

p
q
∴ p ∧ q

Combine two established lines.

Resolution

p ∨ q
¬p ∨ r
∴ q ∨ r

Cancel opposite versions of one proposition.

Also legal: replace any line by a logically equivalent form. State the equivalence used.

2 · Four predicate inference rules

RuleFormSafety condition
Universal instantiation∀x P(x) ∴ P(c)c may be any element in the domain.
Universal generalisationP(c) for arbitrary c ∴ ∀x P(x)c must be arbitrary, not a special witness.
Existential instantiation∃x P(x) ∴ P(c) for some cUse a fresh name for the unknown witness.
Existential generalisationP(c) ∴ ∃x P(x)One established witness is enough.

3 · Goal-first derivation routine

  1. Write the conclusion separately and ask which rule could produce its outermost operator.
  2. Split conjunction premises early; eliminate implications when a useful rule becomes visible.
  3. For quantified premises, instantiate before using propositional rules.
  4. Number every new line and cite the exact earlier lines that justify it.
  5. Stop when the conclusion appears exactly. Do not add an unsupported “therefore”.

4 · Four proof templates

Direct proof of p → q

  1. Assume p.
  2. Unpack definitions.
  3. Use valid algebra or known results.
  4. Reach q.

Assume p. … Therefore q.

Contrapositive proof of p → q

  1. Rewrite the target as ¬q → ¬p.
  2. Assume ¬q.
  3. Derive ¬p.

Assume ¬q. … Therefore ¬p.

Contradiction proof of p

  1. Assume ¬p.
  2. Use definitions and known facts.
  3. Derive r ∧ ¬r, or violate an assumption.
  4. Reject ¬p.

Assume ¬p. … Contradiction. Hence p.

Biconditional proof of p ↔ q

  1. Prove p → q.
  2. Separately prove q → p.
  3. Conclude p ↔ q.

5 · Choosing a method

Signal in the targetFirst method to tryWhy
If p, then qDirectThe hypothesis gives useful structure immediately.
If p, then “not …”ContrapositiveThe negated conclusion may unpack into a positive form.
Impossible existence or irrationalityContradictionAssuming the opposite gives a concrete witness to attack.
p iff qTwo directionsA biconditional literally contains two implications.

Fast error check

  • Affirming the consequent is invalid: p → q, q ∴ p.
  • Denying the antecedent is invalid: p → q, ¬p ∴ ¬q.
  • An existential witness is “some” element, not an arbitrary element.
  • A contradiction must conflict with an assumption or established fact; surprising algebra is not enough.
